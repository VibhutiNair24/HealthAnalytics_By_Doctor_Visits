Exploratory Analysis — 12 Plots with Storytelling

1 Distribution of Doctor Visits (Target Variable)
❝ How often do Australians actually visit the doctor? ❞
Over 55% of patients recorded zero visits in the 2-week observation window. The distribution is heavily right-skewed with a long tail reaching 9 visits. The median is 0, mean is ~0.93. This zero-inflation and skewness means standard linear regression is inappropriate; Poisson, Negative Binomial, or Zero-Inflated models are required for predictive work.
2 Gender Distribution & Visit Rates
❝ Do women visit doctors more than men? ❞
Women have a meaningfully higher mean visit count than men. Men skew more heavily toward zero visits, while women spread across 1–3 visits. This aligns with established healthcare literature: women are more likely to seek preventive care, while men tend to delay or avoid medical attention for moderate symptoms.
3 Age Group & Healthcare Utilisation
❝ Does healthcare demand really grow with age? ❞
There is a clear monotonic increase in mean visits with age. Seniors (62–72) visit nearly twice as often as young adults (19–27). Box plots show both the mean and spread widen with age — a growing cohort of older patients become very heavy users. The ageing population will drive disproportionate future demand on primary care services.
4 Income vs Doctor Visits
❝ Does wealth buy better — or more — healthcare? ❞
Counter-intuitively, the lowest income quintile has the highest visit rate. This is explained by two forces: (1) lower-income individuals have worse baseline health from social determinants; and (2) many qualify for freepoor government insurance, removing the financial barrier. Wealthier patients visit less — likely because they enjoy better preventive health and fewer acute episodes.
5 Illness Count vs Doctor Visits
❝ The most obvious driver — but how strong is the signal? ❞
illness is the single strongest predictor of visits in the dataset (Pearson r ≈ 0.37). Mean visits rise almost linearly from 0.8 (0 illnesses) to 2.5+ (5 illnesses). The crosstab heatmap confirms the clear diagonal shift: patients with 4–5 illnesses almost never have zero visits. Direct, concrete, actionable.
6 Days of Reduced Activity vs Visits
❝ Does illness severity — not just count — drive utilisation? ❞
Days of reduced activity shows a strong step-wise increase in mean visits. Patients impaired for 10–14 days average nearly 3× more visits than those with 0 reduced days. Even within each severity bucket, the distribution remains right-skewed, suggesting access barriers prevent some severely impaired patients from reaching care. Together with illness, reduced forms a powerful two-dimensional severity signal.
7 Self-Rated Health Score vs Visits
❝ Does how people feel about their health predict how much they use it? ❞
Patients who rate their health as poor (higher score) visit significantly more. The health score distribution is bimodal — a large cluster at 0 (excellent) and a secondary peak at higher scores — revealing two sub-populations: a healthy majority who rarely visit, and a sicker minority who visit frequently. Self-rated health is a validated, easy-to-collect predictor.
8 Insurance Type & Healthcare Utilisation
❝ Does the type of insurance coverage shape healthcare demand? ❞
Govt-Poor patients have the highest mean visits, reflecting the combined burden of worse health and now-affordable care. Private insurance holders visit moderately. The zero-visit rate is highest for uninsured patients, suggesting a residual access barrier. Insurance type alone is not a dominant predictor; health status always dominates.
9 Chronic Condition Impact
❝ How much do long-term conditions drive healthcare demand? ❞
Limiting chronic conditions (lchronic) are a high-impact driver. Patients with both chronic types have the highest mean visits of any group. Non-limiting conditions add a modest but consistent increase. The vast majority of the sample has no chronic condition — explaining the heavy zero-inflation. Chronic disease management programmes targeting the 'both' group can significantly reduce acute visit volume.
10 Correlation Heatmap
❝ Which features are most correlated with visits — and with each other? ❞
illness and reduced have the strongest positive correlations with visits. age is moderately positive; income slightly negative. lchronic and nchronic both positively correlate with visits. private and freepoor are mutually exclusive (negatively correlated). illness and reduced also correlate with each other — both measure health burden from complementary angles.
11 Age × Gender Interaction
❝ Do men and women follow the same trajectory with age? ❞
Both genders show an upward trend, but women consistently out-visit men — especially in the 32–52 age range (reproductive and peri-menopausal years). The gender gap narrows in older age groups (67–72) as men's health deteriorates and their visit rates catch up. Women aged 32–57 are the most frequent users relative to their male peers — a key segment for primary care planning.
12 Feature Importance: Visitors vs Non-Visitors
❝ What best separates patients who visit from those who don't? ❞
Ranked by mean absolute difference between visitor and non-visitor populations: illness → reduced → health → lchronic → age are the top 5 discriminators. Demographics (income, gender, insurance type) have smaller but non-trivial effects. This ranking directly informs feature selection for any predictive model built on this dataset.
